# Lecture Script: Tableau — Dashboards That Support Decisions
> **Instructor Reference** — Module 3: Tableau Dashboards + Storytelling | Academic Session 23 | Duration: 2 Hours | Instructor: Balaji

---

## Session Overview
**Goal:** Students can take a clean, well-designed dashboard and adjust it so it supports one specific, nameable decision — by trimming to relevant evidence, visually highlighting the key insight, and aligning charts into a trend-evidence-ask structure.

**Student profile at this point:** They can build clean, decluttered, well-organized dashboards. Everything so far has focused on making a dashboard readable, not on making it persuasive toward one outcome. Expect an instinct toward "completeness" and "fairness" (equal-sized charts) that actively works against decision support, and initial discomfort with the idea of a dashboard visually emphasizing one insight over others.

**Key outcome:** Students should leave able to look at any dashboard and ask, "what one decision is this supposed to help someone make — and can I tell what that decision is in 15 seconds?"

> 🎯 **The one sentence this session must land:** *A dashboard built to display data treats every chart equally; a dashboard built to support a decision is a short, honest argument that leads somewhere.*

## Opening — "The Meeting That Went Nowhere" (8 min)

> "Picture a real meeting: Kirana365's marketing lead presents a dashboard with 12 equally-sized charts — revenue, orders, churn, ad spend, delivery times, ratings, everything. The founder looks at it for thirty seconds and says, 'okay... so what do you want me to decide?'"

Show the actual 12-chart dashboard, cluttered with equal-weight tiles.

> "Everything on this dashboard is true. Everything is well-designed by last session's standards — clean, no clutter, good layout. And it still failed, completely, at its actual job."

**Pivot line:** "Today's session is about that gap — between a dashboard that's clean and readable, and one that actually gets a decision made in the room."

**Context for the sessions ahead:** "This decision-first instinct carries straight into reading dashboards next session, and into writing up insights the session after that — everything from here treats a dashboard as an argument, not a display case."

---

## Concept Block 1: Displaying Data vs Supporting a Decision (10 min)

> "Think of the difference between a shop's full inventory list and a shopkeeper pointing at exactly the three items running low before Diwali. Both are true. Only one helps you decide what to restock right now."

Rebuild the opening's 12-chart dashboard live, trimming it down to the three charts relevant to one specific decision: "should we increase the Bengaluru ad budget by ₹50,000?"

> "Nine charts just disappeared. Nothing about the business changed — I just stopped trying to answer every possible question and started answering the one question actually on the table."

### 🔴 The trap / highest-value moment
> "The trap: believing a 'complete' dashboard, with every available metric, is automatically more useful. Write this down: **for decision support, completeness works against you — it hands the reader your filtering job.**"

## Practical Block 1: The One-Decision Trim (10 min)

Give students a 10-chart Kirana365 dashboard and one specific decision to support: "should we renew our contract with Delivery Partner X?" Task: identify which 3-4 charts are actually relevant and which should be removed.

**Answer key with reasoning:** Delivery time trend, on-time delivery rate, and cost-per-delivery for Partner X (vs alternatives) stay; unrelated charts like customer app-session length or product category mix get removed — the test is whether each kept chart directly answers "should we renew this contract," not merely "is this related to delivery in general."

💬 **Expect pushback**: "Isn't it safer to show everything so nobody can say we hid something?" Welcome it. Say: "You're not hiding data — it's still available on request. You're choosing what to lead with, which is a completely different, and necessary, skill."

---

## Concept Block 2: Highlighting the Key Insight Visually (10 min)

> "A newspaper headline doesn't bury the main story in paragraph six — it's the biggest, boldest text on the page, on purpose."

Live demo: on the trimmed Bengaluru dashboard, make the ROI figure ("₹4.20 return per ₹1 spent") the largest, boldest element, with the supporting trend and comparison charts arranged smaller around it.

> "Ten seconds, and even someone walking past this screen catches the one fact that matters most."

### 🔴 The trap / highest-value moment
> "The trap: giving every chart equal visual weight 'to be fair.' Write this down: **a decision dashboard isn't a neutral museum exhibit — visual hierarchy pointing at the truth is honest, not manipulative, as long as the underlying numbers are accurate.**"

## Practical Block 2: Find and Enlarge the Headline (10 min)

Give students a dashboard where all four charts are equal size, and ask them to identify which single fact is actually the most decision-relevant, then resize the layout so that fact becomes the visually dominant element.

**Answer key with reasoning:** Students should identify the ROI or conversion trend figure (whichever most directly supports the specific decision stated) as the headline, and demonstrate resizing it larger while shrinking supporting context — grading focuses on correct identification of the headline fact, not just visual execution.

💬 **Expect a question about "isn't making one number bigger a form of bias?"** Welcome it. Say: "Bias would be showing a misleading or cherry-picked number bigger. Showing the single most relevant, accurate fact bigger is just good communication — the two are easy to tell apart if you check your numbers honestly."

## BREAK (10 min)

---

## Concept Block 3: Aligning Visuals to Tell a Clear Story (13 min)

> "A decision dashboard should read like a short argument: trend, then evidence, then ask. Not a random collection of charts sitting next to each other."

Build the three-part Bengaluru story live: (1) trend line showing rising conversion over 3 months, (2) KPI comparison showing ₹4.20 ROI vs company average ₹2.80, (3) a simple projection: "₹50,000 increase → projected ₹2,10,000 additional revenue next quarter."

> "Each chart hands off to the next. By the third chart, the reader has already mentally arrived at 'yes, approve this' before I've said a word."

### 🔴 The trap / highest-value moment
> "The trap: including a genuinely interesting chart — like customer satisfaction scores — that doesn't build toward this specific decision. Write this down: **interesting is not the same as relevant to this exact story. Cut it, even if it's a good chart, if it doesn't serve this decision.**"

## Practical Block 3: Build Your Own Trend-Evidence-Ask (12 min)

Give students a new decision scenario: "should Kirana365 open a fifth city (Pune)?" with a pile of relevant and irrelevant charts available. Task: select and sequence three charts into a trend → evidence → ask structure.

**Answer key with reasoning:** A reasonable structure: trend (nearby cities' order growth over recent months), evidence (a comparable existing city's profitability after 6 months of operation), ask (a projected break-even timeline for Pune based on that comparison) — the specific charts matter less than demonstrating the three-step narrative logic.

💬 **Expect an argument about "what if the data doesn't support a clean yes/no?"** Welcome it. Say: "Then the honest ask might be 'run a 3-month pilot before full commitment' — the structure still works, the ask just reflects genuine uncertainty instead of manufacturing false confidence."

---

## Concept Block 4: Reviewing for Decision-Readiness (14 min)

> "Before presenting, run every decision dashboard through four quick questions — don't assume it's ready just because it looks clean."

Walk the four-question checklist on the board, applying each to the finished Bengaluru dashboard: one decision? Yes. Key insight most prominent? Yes. All charts building toward it? Yes. Could a stranger state the recommendation in 15 seconds? Test it live by flashing the dashboard to a student who hasn't seen it.

### 🔴 The trap / highest-value moment
> "The trap: skipping this review because the dashboard is already clean by last session's design standards. Write this down: **clean design and decision-readiness are related but different — a dashboard can look excellent and still lead nowhere.**"

## Practical Block 4: Run the Checklist (13 min)

Students take their trend-evidence-ask dashboard from Practical Block 3 and run it through the four-question checklist themselves, then swap with a partner to test the "15-second stranger test" on each other's work.

**Answer key with reasoning:** A passing dashboard gets a clear "yes" on all four questions, confirmed by the partner correctly stating the intended recommendation without prior context — a failing result on the 15-second test is diagnostic, not a failure grade, and should prompt a specific, nameable fix (usually: headline insight isn't prominent enough, or an irrelevant chart is diluting focus).

💬 **Expect a question about what to do if the checklist reveals a real problem.** Welcome it. Say: "That's the entire point of running it before the real meeting, not during it — better to catch a weak headline or a distracting chart now than in front of the founder."

## Summary & Bridge (5 min)

| Concept | The one thing to remember |
|---|---|
| Data vs decisions | Include only evidence relevant to the one decision at hand |
| Highlighting the key insight | Make the most important fact the most visually prominent element |
| Aligning visuals | Structure charts as trend → evidence → ask, building toward one conclusion |
| Reviewing for readiness | Run the four-question checklist before presenting, every time |

Close on the thesis: "A dashboard built to display data treats every chart equally; a dashboard built to support a decision is a short, honest argument that leads somewhere. Today you built the second kind."

**Bridge to Session 24:** "Next session we switch roles — Reading Dashboards for Insight — practicing how to walk into someone else's dashboard, one you didn't build, and pull out the real story and decision it's arguing for."

## Q&A & Doubt Solving (5 min)

**Q: Is it dishonest to make one chart bigger than the others?**
→ Not if the underlying numbers are accurate and genuinely the most relevant — visual hierarchy pointing at the truth is good communication, not manipulation.

**Q: What if a decision genuinely needs more than three supporting charts?**
→ That's fine — trend-evidence-ask is a minimum structure, not a hard limit; just make sure every additional chart still serves that same one decision.

**Q: Should I remove the "extra" charts entirely, or just move them elsewhere?**
→ Usually move them to a secondary page or a supporting appendix dashboard — the data isn't wrong or useless, it's just not part of this specific argument.

**Q: How do I know which decision a dashboard should be built around if my manager hasn't specified one?**
→ Ask directly before building — "what decision will this help you make?" is a completely reasonable, expected question from an analyst, and usually saves significant rework.

**Q: Can a decision-support dashboard ever recommend "no" or "not yet"?**
→ Absolutely — the goal is an honest, clear argument, not always a "yes." A well-built dashboard that clearly argues against a bad idea is just as valuable.

## Instructor Notes
- **Words not yet earned:** "narrative visualization," "pre-attentive attributes," "cognitive load" — these are real design/communication concepts but beyond today's working vocabulary; stick to trend, evidence, ask, and headline insight.
- **Biggest risk in this session:** Students conflating "highlighting one insight" with bias or dishonesty — spend real time distinguishing "emphasizing the true, relevant fact" from "cherry-picking or misleading," since this distinction is the ethical core of the session.
- **Board management:** Keep the four-question review checklist and the trend → evidence → ask structure visible for the entire session.
- **Common confusions:**
  1. Believing more charts and more "completeness" always means a better dashboard.
  2. Treating equal visual weight across charts as the fair or neutral choice.
  3. Assuming a clean, well-designed dashboard is automatically decision-ready without the review step.
- **Cross-references:** This session's trend-evidence-ask structure is exactly what students practice identifying from the reader's side in Session 24 (Reading Dashboards for Insight), and becomes the basis for the written narrative in Session 25 (Insight Writing with GenAI).
- **Local/cultural context notes:** The Diwali-restocking and newspaper-headline analogies land well with this cohort; keep the Bengaluru ad-budget scenario as the throughline example, since a concrete rupee-based ROI figure (₹4.20 returned per ₹1 spent) makes the "make the headline prominent" lesson tangible rather than abstract.
